1. One numerical variable → Histogram

Example: book prices

plt.hist(df["price"])

Question:

"How are book prices distributed?"

You want to see ranges:

$0-10     → many books
$10-20    → many books
$20-30    → few books
$30-40    → very few

Histogram = distribution of numerical data.

2. One categorical variable → Bar chart

Example: book genre

df["genre"].value_counts().plot(kind="bar")

Question:

"How many books are in each genre?"

Fiction   → 400
Fantasy   → 250
Romance   → 180

Bar chart = compare categories.

3. Two numerical variables → Scatter plot

Example:

price vs pages

plt.scatter(df["pages"], df["price"])

Question:

"Do books with more pages tend to have higher prices?"

Scatter = relationship between two numerical variables.

4. One categorical + one numerical → Box plot / Bar chart

Example:

genre vs price

df.boxplot(column="price", by="genre")

Question:

"How does price differ across genres?"

You're comparing the numerical variable (price) between categories (genre).

5. Two categorical variables → Bar chart / Crosstab

Example:

genre vs author

pd.crosstab(df["genre"], df["author"])

Or visualize counts with a bar chart.

Question:

"How are authors distributed across genres?"

6. Numerical variable + time → Line chart

Example:

year vs average book price

df.groupby("year")["price"].mean().plot(kind="line")

Question:

"How has average book price changed over time?"

Line = trend/progression, especially time.

🧠 Your cheat sheet
What you have	What you want to know	Graph
1 numerical	Distribution	Histogram
1 categorical	Compare categories/counts	Bar chart
2 numerical	Relationship	Scatter plot
Categorical + numerical	Compare numbers between groups	Box plot / Bar
2 categorical	Compare category combinations	Bar / Crosstab
Numerical + time	Trend over time	Line chart
